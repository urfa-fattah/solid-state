1Crystalline vs amorphous solids

Every solid is made of atoms, but the atoms are not arranged the same way in every solid. How regular the arrangement is gives the first and most basic classification of solids.

TypeOrderWhat it meansExamples
CrystallineLong-range order Atoms repeat in a regular pattern over a very large distance, across millions of atoms. If you know the pattern in one place, you can predict it far away. NaCl, Si, diamond, metals
AmorphousShort-range order Only the first few neighbours of an atom are arranged regularly. Farther away, the regularity fades out. glass, plastics

Your note draws it as a row of dots that keeps going (o o o o o …) for long-range order, and only a few dots (o o o) for short-range order.

Why it mattersadded

Because of the repeating pattern, a crystalline solid melts at one sharp temperature. An amorphous solid, such as glass, instead softens gradually over a range of temperatures.

Keywords explained

Solid
A state of matter in which atoms stay close together around fixed average positions, so the material keeps its own shape.
Crystal / crystalline solid
A solid whose atoms form a regular pattern that repeats in all three directions.
Amorphous solid
A solid with no repeating pattern over long distances. (The word means ‘without form’.)
Order
How regularly atoms are arranged.
Long-range order
The pattern is kept across the whole solid, over many atom-spacings. The signature of a crystal.
Short-range order
The pattern holds only among nearby neighbours. The signature of an amorphous solid.
Periodic
Repeating again and again at equal steps.

Sources: Class note p.1; [1] Ch. 1, Ch. 19; [3]

2Lattice, basis and unit cell

A crystal is described with two ideas: a lattice and a basis.

Crystal structure = lattice + basis

Think of wallpaper. The lattice is the grid of positions; the basis is the picture printed at every position. The same lattice with a different basis gives a different crystal.

Unit cell and its types

The unit cell is the small building block of the crystal; stacking copies of it in all directions builds the whole crystal. Unit cells are grouped by where their lattice points are:

Cell typeExtra lattice pointsLattice points per cell
Primitivenone (corners only)8 × 1/8 = 1
Body-centeredone at the centre of the cell1 + 1 = 2
Face-centeredone at the centre of each of the 6 faces1 + 6 × 1/2 = 4
Base-centeredone at the centre of two opposite faces1 + 2 × 1/2 = 2

Bravais latticeadded

Combining the 7 crystal systems with the primitive, body-, face- and base-centered types gives exactly 14 distinct lattices, called Bravais lattices. (See the table in Section 3 for how they split among the systems.)

Keywords explained

Lattice
An endless, regularly spaced array of points in space. It is a geometric idea; there are no atoms in it.
Lattice point
One point of the lattice. This is where a copy of the basis is placed.
Basis
The atom, or group of atoms, attached to every lattice point. Example: in NaCl the basis is one Na and one Cl.
Crystal structure
The real arrangement of atoms that results from lattice and basis together.
Unit cell
The repeating piece of the crystal which, shifted along three directions, rebuilds the whole crystal. The cube drawn in pictures is usually the conventional unit cell.
Primitive cell
A unit cell holding exactly one lattice point, the smallest possible cell.
Centered cell
A unit cell with extra lattice points besides the corners: at the body centre, the face centres, or the base. Such cells are larger than primitive ones but have a simpler shape.

Sources: Class note p.1; [1] Ch. 1 (Basis and the Crystal Structure; Fundamental Types of Lattices); [2] Ch. 4; [10]

3Crystal systems

The shape of a unit cell is fixed by six numbers called lattice parameters: the three edge lengths a, b, c and the three angles α, β, γ between them.

  • a, b, c = lengths of the edges along the x, y, z directions.
  • α = angle between b and c; β = angle between a and c; γ = angle between a and b.

Your note covers two systems in detail:

Keywords explained

Lattice parameters
The six numbers that fix the shape and size of the unit cell: a, b, c, α, β, γ.
Lattice constant
In a cubic crystal, the edge length a. All three edges are equal, so one number is enough. Usually quoted in Å (1 Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m).
Crystal system
A class of crystals defined by the relations among the lattice parameters (which edges are equal, which angles are 90°). There are 7.
Cubic
All three edges equal and all angles 90°: a perfect cube.
Hexagonal
Two edges equal (a = b), the third (c) different; the angle between a and b is 120°.
Edge
One side of the unit cell, the line between two corners.

Sources: Class note p.1; [1] Ch. 1 (Fundamental Types of Lattices); [2] Ch. 4; [10]

4Cubic system: SC, BCC, FCC

The cubic system has three common unit cells. In the drawings, blue atoms are at corners, orange at the body centre, and green at the face centres.

Diagonals: the directions where atoms touch

In BCC and FCC the atoms touch along a diagonal, not along an edge, so you need the lengths of two diagonals. By the Pythagorean theorem:

Comparing the three cells

CellAtoms sit atAtoms per cellCoordination numberAtoms touch alonga in terms of rNearest-neighbour distance
SCcorners16edgea = 2ra
BCCcorners + body centre28body diagonal√3·a = 4r√3·a / 2
FCCcorners + face centres412face diagonal√2·a = 4ra / √2

In every case the nearest-neighbour distance equals 2r, because the two closest atoms touch.

Keywords explained

Simple cubic (SC)
Atoms at the 8 corners only. Also called primitive cubic.
Body-centered cubic (BCC)
Atoms at the 8 corners plus one extra atom at the very centre of the cube.
Face-centered cubic (FCC)
Atoms at the 8 corners plus one at the centre of each of the 6 faces.
Corner atom / face-centre atom / body-centre atom
The atom sits on a corner of the cube, in the middle of a face, or at the centre inside the cube.
Atomic radius (r)
The radius of an atom treated as a hard sphere. Two touching atoms have centres 2r apart.
Coordination number (CN)
The number of nearest neighbour atoms that one atom touches directly.
Nearest-neighbour distance
The distance between the centres of an atom and its closest neighbour.
Face diagonal
The line across one face, from a corner to the opposite corner; length √2·a.
Body diagonal
The line through the inside of the cube from one corner to the farthest corner; length √3·a.

Sources: Class note pp.1–2; [1] Ch. 1 (Simple Crystal Structures); [3]. The nearest-neighbour formulas follow from the geometry; they are not in the note.

An atom on a corner or a face is shared with neighbouring cells, so only a fraction of it belongs to this cell.

BCC: 8 × 1/8 + 1 = 2

The pink margin note says "corner + body centre = 4 atom". That label is a slip: FCC is corner + face centre: 1 + (6 × 1/2 = 3) = 4. Body centre belongs to BCC.

Keywords explained

Shared atom
An atom sitting on the boundary of more than one unit cell, so each cell ‘owns’ only a fraction of it.
Atoms per unit cell (n)
The total you get after adding up all fractions that belong to one cell (also called the effective number). You need it for density and packing fraction.

Sources: Class note pp.1–3; [3]; [1] Ch. 1

6Packing fraction

Packing fraction tells you what part of the unit cell's volume is filled by atoms. Atoms are treated as hard spheres of radius r that touch each other.

Packing fraction = (volume of atoms in the cell) ÷ (volume of the cell) = n · (4/3)π r³ ÷ a³

Step 1: express a in terms of r (where atoms touch)

Why is the diagonal 4r? Walking along the diagonal you cross half a corner atom (r), one whole middle atom (2r), and half of the atom at the far end (r): r + 2r + r = 4r.

Step 2: substitute and calculate

SC (n = 1, a = 2r)

PF = (4/3 π r³) ÷ (2r)³ = (4/3 π r³) ÷ 8r³ = π/6 = 0.5236 → 52.36%

The remaining ≈ 47.64% of the cell is empty space (void).

BCC (n = 2, a = 4r/√3)

PF = 2 × (4/3 π r³) ÷ (4r/√3)³ = (8/3 π r³) ÷ (64 r³ / 3√3) = π√3/8 = 0.6802 → ≈ 68.0%

FCC (n = 4, a = 4r/√2 = 2√2·r)

PF = 4 × (4/3 π r³) ÷ (2√2·r)³ = (16/3 π r³) ÷ (16√2·r³) = π/(3√2) = 0.7405 → ≈ 74.0%
CellPacking fractionEmpty fraction (1 − PF)CN

FCC0.74050.259512
Order to remember: FCC (74%) > BCC (68%) > SC (52%). More atoms per cell and more touching neighbours (CN 12 > 8 > 6) mean tighter packing.

An extra factadded

Packing equal spheres as tightly as possible gives π/(3√2) ≈ 74.05%. Both FCC and hexagonal close-packed (hcp) reach this maximum, which is why they are called close-packed structures. The proof that nothing denser exists is the Kepler conjecture, proved by Hales in 2005.

Keywords explained

Packing fraction (PF)
Volume of atoms ÷ volume of the unit cell. A number between 0 and 1, or a percentage. Also called atomic packing factor (APF).
Hard-sphere model
Treating atoms as solid spheres that cannot be squeezed, which keeps the geometry simple.
Volume of a sphere
For radius r, volume = (4/3)π r³.
Volume of the unit cell
For a cube it is a³, where a is the lattice constant.
Void
The empty space between atoms. Void fraction = 1 − PF.
Close-packed
A structure in which equal spheres are packed as densely as possible (74.05%).

Sources: Class note pp.2–3; [1] Ch. 1; [3]; [11] (Kepler conjecture). The note has BCC 68.01% and FCC 74.04%; the exact values are 68.02% and 74.05% (a rounding difference).

7Miller indices

Miller indices (h k l) name a plane in a crystal with three small whole numbers. Atoms are arranged differently on different planes, so planes must be identified.

Three steps

  1. Intercepts: note where the plane cuts the x, y and z axes (use the cell edge as the unit). If the plane is parallel to an axis, that intercept is ∞.
  2. Reciprocals: invert each intercept (1/∞ = 0).
  3. Clear fractions: if any fraction remains, multiply all three by the smallest number that makes them whole numbers.
Example1. Intercepts2. Reciprocals3. Whole numbers(h k l)
Your note's example(1, 2, 1)(1, 1/2, 1)× 2(2 1 2)
Example 2 (added)(2, 1, ∞)(1/2, 1, 0)× 2(1 2 0)
Example 3 (added)(1/2, 1, 1/3)(2, 1, 3)not needed(2 1 3)

Why take reciprocals? When a plane is parallel to an axis, its intercept is infinite, which is awkward to use. Taking the reciprocal turns it into a simple 0.

Planes of the cube

Axes: x points right, y goes into the page, z points up. The shaded area is the plane.

More notationadded

  • Negative index: a negative intercept is written with a bar over the number, for example (1 1̄ 0).
  • {h k l}: in a cubic crystal, planes that are equivalent by symmetry, such as (100), (010) and (001), form a family written {100}.
  • [u v w]: square brackets name a direction, not a plane. In a cubic crystal the direction [h k l] is perpendicular to the plane (h k l).

Keywords explained

Plane
A flat layer of atoms inside the crystal.
Axis (x, y, z)
The three lines drawn from one corner of the unit cell along its three edges; they are the reference for measuring.
Intercept
The point where the plane cuts an axis, measured as a fraction or multiple of the cell edge.
Reciprocal
The inverse of a number: the reciprocal of x is 1/x.
Miller indices (h k l)
The three smallest whole numbers in the ratio of the reciprocal intercepts. This is the name of the plane.
Parallel plane
A plane that never meets an axis. Its intercept is ∞, so its index is 0.

Sources: Class note pp.3, 4, 6; [1] Ch. 1 (Index System for Crystal Planes); [5] (origin of the notation); [3]

8Single crystal vs polycrystal

  • Single crystal: one continuous lattice with the same orientation throughout the sample.
  • Polycrystal: built from many small crystals, each with its own orientation. These small crystals are the grains. In other words, every grain inside a polycrystal is itself a single crystal.
  • The surfaces where grains meet are called grain boundaries.

An extra factadded

Properties of a single crystal usually depend on direction (anisotropic). In a polycrystal the grains point in random directions, so on average the properties come out almost the same in every direction (isotropic).

Keywords explained

Single crystal
A sample made of one crystal, with no grain boundaries inside.
Polycrystal
A sample made of many small crystals (grains) joined together.
Grain
One single-crystal region inside a polycrystal.
Grain boundary
The interface between two grains where the lattice direction changes abruptly.
Orientation
The direction in which the lattice axes point.
Anisotropic / isotropic
Anisotropic: properties change with direction. Isotropic: the same in every direction.

Sources: Class note p.4; [3]; [1] Ch. 1, Ch. 19

Real crystals are never perfect. Any departure from the perfect pattern is a defect. Defects are classified by their dimension:

Point defects: Frenkel and Schottky

FrenkelSchottky
What happensThe atom moves to an interstitial position inside the crystal.The atoms leave the lattice and the crystal.
DensityStays the same (nothing is lost)Decreases (mass drops, volume stays about the same)

Memory hook: Frenkel = the atom moves, so density is unchanged. Schottky = atoms leave, so density drops.

In an ionic crystal a Schottky defect is usually a missing cation–anion pair, so the crystal stays electrically neutral. That is why two vacancies are drawn (one small site, one large site). added

Line and plane defectsadded

Keywords explained

Crystal defect
Any departure from the perfect repeating pattern.
Point defect
A defect confined to one or two atom sites.
Line defect
A defect extending along a line, such as a dislocation.
Plane defect
A defect extending across a surface, such as a grain boundary.
Vacancy
A lattice site that should hold an atom but is empty.
Interstitial position
A gap between the regular atom sites where no atom normally sits. In a defect an atom can squeeze in here.
Frenkel defect
A vacancy plus an interstitial atom created from the same atom. Density does not change.
Schottky defect
An atom (an ion pair in an ionic crystal) leaves the lattice and the crystal, leaving vacancies. Density decreases.
Dislocation
A line error in the atom rows, where an extra half-plane is stuck in. It lets crystals deform (bend) under force.
Ionic crystal / cation / anion
An ionic crystal is built from positive ions (cations) and negative ions (anions), such as K⁺ and Cl⁻.
Density
Mass per unit volume: ρ = mass ÷ volume.

10Band theory of solids

Starting point: electrons in one atom

An electron in an isolated atom can only have certain definite energies, never the values in between. Silicon (atomic number 14) has 14 electrons arranged as:

Si (14) = 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p²
  • Read each piece as shell number + letter + count: in 1s², 1 is the shell (n = 1), s is the subshell type, and the superscript 2 is the number of electrons in it.
  • Check: 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 2 = 14 ✓. The outermost shell (n = 3) holds 3s² 3p², that is 4 valence electrons.
  • The 1st, 2nd and 3rd levels in your note's drawing are the energy levels of the shells n = 1, 2, 3.

From levels to bands

A silicon single crystal contains an enormous number of atoms (the note writes ~10²⁴). By the Pauli exclusion principle two electrons cannot occupy the same state, so when atoms come close each single level splits into a huge number of extremely close levels. That cluster is an energy band: a band of energy.

Outer electrons overlap more with neighbouring atoms, so their bands are wider. Inner electrons stay tied to their own atom, so their bands are narrow.

What comes nextadded

The note stops here. The next ideas in this chapter: the highest filled band is the valence band, the empty band above it is the conduction band, and the forbidden energy region between them is the band gap (Eg). The size of that gap decides whether a material is a conductor, a semiconductor or an insulator.

Keywords explained

Energy level
A definite energy that an electron in an isolated atom is allowed to have.
Electron configuration
The list of how many electrons sit in each shell and subshell, e.g. 1s² 2s² …
Shell / subshell (s, p)
Shell: a layer labelled n = 1, 2, 3 … Subshell: a sub-layer inside a shell (s, p, d …). An s subshell holds at most 2 electrons, a p subshell at most 6.
Valence electron
An electron in the outermost shell; these decide bonding and electrical behaviour. Si has 4.
Pauli exclusion principle
No two electrons can be in the same quantum state. This is why levels split into bands.
Energy band
A nearly continuous range of energy made of a huge number of very close levels.
Valence band
The band filled by the valence electrons; usually the highest filled band at 0 K.
Conduction band
The band above the valence band. Electrons here move freely and carry current.
Band gap (Eg)
The energy difference between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band, where no levels exist.
eV (electron-volt)
A unit of energy: what one electron gains moving through 1 volt. 1 eV ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
Conductor / semiconductor / insulator
Conductor: overlapping bands, conducts well. Semiconductor: small gap. Insulator: large gap, does not conduct.

Sources: Class note pp.5, 7; [1] Ch. 7 (Energy Bands), Ch. 8 (Semiconductor Crystals); [4] (Si band gap, valence/conduction band). The ~10²⁴ figure is from the note; in practice Si holds about 5 × 10²² atoms per cm³.

11Worked problem: KCl lattice constant

Problem. The density of a KCl crystal is 1.98 × 10³ kg/m³. Find the lattice constant and the distance between two adjacent atoms. The lattice is face-centered cubic (FCC).

Plan

Density = mass ÷ volume. If we find the mass of one unit cell, and the density is given, we get a³, and then a.

  1. Mass of one formula unit. Molar mass M = 39 + 35.5 = 74.5 g/mol. Avogadro number N = 6.023 × 10²³ per mol. So one KCl has mass 74.5 ÷ (6.023 × 10²³) g.
  2. How many KCl in one cell? The KCl lattice is FCC, and an FCC cell has 4 lattice points. One K–Cl pair (the basis) sits on each point, so the cell holds 4 KCl (4 K⁺ + 4 Cl⁻).
  3. Mass of the unit cell.
    m = 4 × 74.5 ÷ (6.023 × 10²³) g = 4.95 × 10⁻²² g = 4.95 × 10⁻²⁵ kg
  4. Lattice constant. From ρ = m ÷ a³,
    a = (m/ρ)1/3 = (4.95 × 10⁻²⁵ ÷ 1.98 × 10³)1/3 = (2.50 × 10⁻²⁸)1/3 ≈ 6.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = 6.3 Å
  5. Distance between adjacent atoms (unfinished in the note, completed here). In KCl, K⁺ and Cl⁻ alternate along a cell edge, so the neighbour distance is
    d = a/2 ≈ 3.15 Å
    Two atoms of the same kind (K–K or Cl–Cl) are closest along a face diagonal: a/√2 ≈ 4.45 Å.
Sanity check: the measured value is a ≈ 6.29 Å (629.2 pm). Our 6.3 Å differs by less than 0.2%, so the calculation is reliable.

General formula (added)

ρ = n · M ÷ (NA · a³)  ⇒  a = [ n · M ÷ (ρ · NA) ]1/3

Here n = formula units per cell, M = molar mass (kg/mol), NA = the Avogadro constant. The note uses 6.023 × 10²³; the exact SI value is 6.02214076 × 10²³. The answer does not change at 2 significant figures.

Keywords explained

Formula unit
The smallest ratio-unit of an ionic compound, e.g. KCl = one K and one Cl.
Molar mass (M)
The mass of one mole of a substance. For KCl: 39 + 35.5 = 74.5 g/mol.
Avogadro number (N)
The number of particles in one mole: ≈ 6.022 × 10²³.
Mole
The unit for amount of substance; one mole holds an Avogadro number of particles.
Lattice constant
The edge length a of the cubic cell (see Section 3).
Å (angstrom)
A unit of length: 1 Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m, about the size of an atom.
Significant figures
The number of reliable digits in a number. An answer is usually given with as many digits as the given data.

Quick self-test

  1. Why does a Schottky defect lower density while a Frenkel defect does not?
  2. Derive the SC packing fraction from a = 2r without looking.
  3. Find the Miller indices of a plane with intercepts (2, 1, ∞).
  4. What is the relation between a and r for BCC, and why?
  5. How many atoms does an FCC cell hold? Show the count.
Show answers
  1. Frenkel: the atom only moves, so mass and volume stay the same and density is unchanged. Schottky: atoms leave the crystal, mass drops while volume stays about the same, so density falls.
  2. PF = (4/3 π r³) ÷ (2r)³ = π/6 ≈ 0.5236 (52.36%).
  3. Reciprocals (1/2, 1, 0) → multiply by 2 → (1 2 0).
  4. √3·a = 4r, so a = 4r/√3. In BCC the atoms touch along the body diagonal, whose length is √3·a and which holds r + 2r + r = 4r.
  5. 8 × 1/8 + 6 × 1/2 = 1 + 3 = 4.
